The process

Eight steps from idea to container

Every one of these has a deliverable you can see. Nothing goes into a black box for six weeks.

Drawing or idea

Send a DWG, DXF, STEP or PDF — or a physical sample, a competitor's product photo, or a dimensioned sketch on paper. Many of our best programmes started as a phone photo and a target price.

Feasibility review

Our engineers check whether it can actually be extruded and fabricated: wall thickness balance, tongue ratios, corner radii, welding access, assembly logic and how the finish will behave. If something will be expensive forever, you hear it now.

Quotation

Tooling cost, unit price, MOQ and lead time, itemised. Where a design change would meaningfully cut cost, we quote both versions so the choice is yours.

Mold / prototype

Dies are designed and cut in-house, and fabrication fixtures built alongside. First articles come off production tooling, not a one-off prototype process that behaves differently at volume.

Sample approval

You receive samples to measure, load test, fit to your cushions and put in front of your buyers. Changes are made and re-sampled before the line is committed.

Mass production

Scheduled across the plants that best suit the process mix and your delivery date, with production status reported as the run progresses.

Quality inspection

Incoming material, in-process, welding, dimensional, surface finish, packaging and pre-shipment checks — documented, with photographic reports.

Shipping

Packed, labelled, documented and despatched from China or Cambodia, whichever suits your duty and logistics planning.

Engineering support

The review that saves you money

Most cost problems in outdoor furniture are designed in, not manufactured in. By the time a die exists, the unit price is largely decided.

So the feasibility review is not a formality. We look for wall thickness that is heavier than the load requires, sections that could be consolidated, weld joints that could be eliminated, and finish specifications that are fighting the geometry.

You are free to ignore every suggestion — plenty of customers have design reasons we cannot see from a drawing. But you will have seen the trade-off before you pay for tooling.

  • Extrudability and tooling risk assessed before cutting a die
  • Part consolidation and weld reduction proposed where possible
  • Material and temper matched to the actual load case
  • Finish specified for the environment, not a generic standard
  • Packaging reviewed against container cube and drop-test needs
